# Validity of the MBTI: what the evidence shows

Research strand 02 of the MBTI literature review. Compiled 2026-10-31. Neither a debunking nor a defence.

Method note: several primary papers (Nature Human Behaviour, PDFs, PubMed abstracts) were not fully retrievable in this session. Each source below is marked with how much I actually saw. Numbers are given only where a source I opened or a search result stated them. Where a number comes from Wikipedia or a blog summarising a paper, that is flagged, and the underlying paper should be checked before the repo relies on it.

Evidence grades: [Strong] replicated across independent samples; [Moderate]; [Weak] single study or publisher-only; [Contested] credible sources disagree.

## Bottom line

The four MBTI scales do overlap meaningfully with four of the Big Five traits, and that overlap has been replicated, but the instrument has no scale for Neuroticism and its "type" claim (people fall into distinct categories) lacks support in score distributions and retest data. There is no solid published evidence that MBTI type predicts job performance or team outcomes, or that it adds anything over Big Five measures. The test's own publisher says it should not be used for hiring or selection. Data-driven clustering of Big Five scores (Gerlach et al. 2018) found a small number of types, not 16, and that result is itself disputed. Much of the positive literature is publisher-linked or small-sample, and some of the strongest critiques are reviews rather than new data.

## Findings

### 1. Convergent validity with the Big Five

- The four MBTI scales map onto four Big Five traits: E-I with Extraversion, S-N with Openness, T-F with Agreeableness, J-P with Conscientiousness. McCrae and Costa (1989) found this in 267 men and 201 women (N = 468, aged 19 to 93) using the NEO-PI with self-reports and peer ratings. [Strong; replicated, see Furnham below] (Sources 1, 2)
- Furnham (1996), 160 adults, NEO-PI vs MBTI: Extraversion tracked E-I closely; Openness correlated with all four MBTI scales, most with S-N; Agreeableness only with T-F; Conscientiousness with T-F and J-P; Neuroticism with none. Later work by Furnham and colleagues with about 900 participants was reported to show the same pattern. Coefficients for these were not available to me. [Strong for the pattern; coefficients unverified] (Source 3, search-result abstract only)
- A secondary citation of McCrae and Costa's values reports E-I r about -.74 with Extraversion and S-N r about .72 with Openness, with weaker T-F/Agreeableness (about .44) and J-P/Conscientiousness (about -.49). This comes from a 2022 paper's summary of the original, not the original. [Moderate; unverified numbers] (Source 4, search result only)
- Neuroticism is what the MBTI misses. In McCrae and Costa's table as reproduced on Wikipedia, Neuroticism correlations with the MBTI scales were small: E-I .16, S-N -.06, T-F .06, J-P .11. Wikipedia also notes Myers, Quenk and Kirby (1995) argued the MBTI's comfort-discomfort dimension is not Neuroticism; I did not read that paper. [Strong that no MBTI scale tracks Neuroticism; Contested whether this matters for the publisher's "comfort-discomfort" add-on] (Sources 3, 5)
- McCrae and Costa also concluded the data did not support truly dichotomous preferences, and that Jung's theory is either wrong or not captured by the MBTI. Whether that is a flaw in the MBTI or in Jung's theory is the authors' interpretation. The authors sell the NEO inventory, a possible conflict of interest raised by one commentator (not in the paper). [Moderate] (Source 1)

### 2. Reliability (needed to interpret any validity claim)

- Type-category stability is poor in older studies: Wikipedia, summarising Pittenger (1993, Review of Educational Research), reports 39 to 76 percent of respondents got a different type after about five weeks; Harvey (1996, Form G) is reported to show about 50 percent keeping the same overall type within nine months and 36 percent after longer. I did not read these originals. [Moderate; secondary] (Source 5)
- The publisher reports scale-level reliability of .80 to .87 across studies (Capraro and Capraro 2002, with T-F lower, .64 to .87) and a 2018 MBTI Manual test-retest of 1,721 adults at 6 to 15 weeks, coefficients .81 to .86. Both are publisher-reported; the Manual is a publisher document. [Weak as independent evidence; Contested vs the type-stability findings] (Source 6)
- Important distinction: scale scores can be reliable while the dichotomised four-letter type is not, because cutoffs fall in the middle of a unimodal distribution. Wikipedia summarises the Capraro and Capraro review as finding only 7 percent of 210 studies reported reliability directly. A search-result note says Bess and Harvey (2002, J Personality Assessment 78:176-186) examined bimodality; the later audit read its abstract, which attributes earlier bimodality to a scoring default and reports center-weighted scores (see 05-claim-audit.md). [Contested] (Sources 5, 7)
- A systematic review (Randall, Isaacson and Ciro 2017) found 221 candidate studies, 7 meeting inclusion criteria, and could pool only 3 test-retest studies, with substantial heterogeneity; it reports reasonable construct validity from the included studies. Seen in a search-result abstract only. [Moderate] (Source 8)

### 3. Predictive validity: job performance, career choice, satisfaction

- No meta-analysis of MBTI type vs job performance that I could open exists in the sources I found. Secondary summaries (Wikipedia) cite Pittenger (1993) and Nowack (1996) as finding poor predictive validity for performance ratings, and a US National Academy of Sciences committee (1991) as finding insufficient well-designed research to justify MBTI use in career counselling. Gardner and Martinko (1996) are cited as saying no definitive conclusion on type and managerial effectiveness can be drawn. I did not read these originals. [Weak to Moderate; absence of positive evidence rather than a demonstrated null] (Source 5)
- For contrast, Big Five Conscientiousness has a long meta-analytic record (Barrick and Mount 1991 is the standard cite; a secondary page gives r about .31 uncorrected, .39 corrected). The Big Five figures were not read from the paper. [Strong that Big Five predicts performance; figures unverified] (search results only)
- Pittenger (2005) is repeatedly cited as saying there is no published demonstration of incremental validity of the MBTI over other personality measures. I only saw secondary descriptions of this. [Weak; secondary] (search result only; see Could not verify)
- Career choice: type does cluster by occupation (the publisher sells MBTI Type Tables for Occupations, 2nd ed., 404 tables, using a self-selection ratio, the share of a type in an occupation divided by its share in the total sample). A 2020 IT student study and a 2001 pharmacist study are small and show type linked to field choice but not to job satisfaction. Occupational clustering shows self-selection, not that type predicts success. The publisher's Type Tables are sold separately and I could not see them. [Weak; publisher and small samples] (Source 9, search results only)
- Publisher-side claim: Moyle and Hackston (2018, J Personality Assessment) argue for "experiential validity" (users find results useful). Seen only via the publisher's summary; no validity coefficients on that page. [Weak] (Source 6)

### 4. Team and organisational outcomes

- I found no meta-analysis of MBTI and team performance. The closest rigorous work is Big Five-based: Bell (2007, J Applied Psychology) found team minimum Agreeableness and team mean Conscientiousness and Openness among the stronger predictors in field studies, with negligible personality effects in lab studies. Abstract only, via a search result. [Moderate for Big Five; none for MBTI] (Source 10)
- Small MBTI team studies exist: a Singapore University of Technology and Design study reported a weak but significant relation between MBTI diversity and project grades; the publisher's own survey of 883 participants found individual type unrelated to team performance, while people unlike the rest of their team perceived team performance as worse. Both seen only through search summaries. [Weak; publisher-run in one case] (Source 11, search result only)

### 5. Hiring use: publisher guidance

- The Myers & Briggs Foundation's ethical-use page states the instrument is not designed for hiring, does not measure capability or job performance, and is meant for self-understanding and development. The Myers-Briggs Company (CPP) blog is described in search results as calling hiring use unethical and saying it may revoke purchase rights for misuse (the original clause was not opened). The MBTI Manual (Myers et al. 1998) is reported by Wikipedia to discourage predicting job success. [Strong that the publisher's stated position is no hiring use] (Sources 5, 12)
- Despite this, use in hiring is reported: Wikipedia cites press reports of Korean employers asking for MBTI results (60 percent of surveyed Korean job-seekers in their twenties opposed it). [Weak; press-sourced] (Source 5)
- Stein and Swan (2019) say the publisher claim of "descriptive, not predictive" sidesteps a requirement for a personality theory, and they cite Coffield et al. (2004) quoting Pittenger that one-third to one-half of published MBTI material appeared in conference proceedings or a journal run by advocates. Judgment, not data. [Contested] (Source 13, blog summary)

### 6. Barnum/Forer effects

- The effect: people rate vague, general descriptions as accurate for themselves, more so when told the text was personalised and when it is mostly flattering. Forer (1949) is the origin; Furnham and Schofield (1987) is the standard review. These details come from search-result summaries, not the originals. [Strong for the general effect; Weak for MBTI specifically] (Source 14, search results)
- I found no experiment that gave randomised or fake MBTI type descriptions and measured acceptance, so whether Barnum acceptance explains the MBTI's popularity is untested in the material I found. The claim circulates as an assertion. [Weak] (search results)
- The MBTI's positive-by-design framing of each type (all preferences described as strengths) is the feature most compatible with high acceptance. This is my inference, not a sourced finding.

### 7. Data-driven clusters (Gerlach et al. 2018)

- Gerlach, Farb, Revelle and Amaral (2018) applied Gaussian mixture models to Big Five scores from four datasets totalling over 1.5 million people (US and England). Of 13 candidate clusters, four survived a density test against randomised data: Average, Reserved, Self-centred, Role Model. Role Model is low in Neuroticism (mean z = -0.70) and relatively high on the other traits. Descriptions of the Average cluster differ between press material and a SIOP commentary. I did not read the full paper. [Moderate; replicated within their four datasets, not independently] (Source 15, search results and PubMed abstract via search)
- The result is disputed. Katahira et al. (2020, Frontiers in Big Data) argue skewed unimodal data with dependence between factors can produce spurious clusters, backed by simulation. In one simulation 45.5 percent of simulated data points fell in the "meaningful" clusters; they cite a reanalysis (Freudenstein et al. 2019) finding 42 percent of respondents in the four clusters. Gerlach et al. published a reply (2019); I only saw the citation. Kerber, Roth and Herzberg (2021, PLoS One, N = 22,820) found five types in German panel data and treat types and dimensions as complementary. [Contested] (Sources 16, 17)
- Comparison with the MBTI: Gerlach's clusters are derived from Big Five traits, number four rather than 16, and are not the same as MBTI types. They are built from Neuroticism-inclusive data. A direct comparison between MBTI types and these clusters was not found. SIOP's commentary contrasts them with schemes like the MBTI's 16 types. [Weak on the comparison] (Source 15, search results)

## What MBTI proponents argue (strongest honest version)

- Scale scores, treated as continuous, have acceptable internal consistency and retest reliability (publisher: .81 to .86 over 6 to 15 weeks in 1,721 adults; Capraro and Capraro reliability generalisation .80 to .87 with T-F lower). The "type unstable" critique mostly targets dichotomised categories, which is also what Big Five researchers say about dichotomising. (Source 6)
- Four MBTI scales do correlate with four Big Five domains; the instrument is therefore not noise. A systematic review (Randall et al. 2017) found reasonable construct validity in the studies it could include. (Sources 1, 3, 8)
- The MBTI is positioned as a development and self-understanding tool, not a selection or predictive tool, and the publisher explicitly bans hiring use. Judging it by job-performance prediction criticises a use the publisher does not endorse. Moyle and Hackston (2018) argue experiential validity matters. Caveat: Stein and Swan argue prediction is still required of a personality theory. (Sources 6, 12, 13)
- Neuroticism omission is deliberate: the publisher treats MBTI as describing normal preferences, not emotional stability, and Myers, Quenk and Kirby (1995) argued the comfort-discomfort scale is not Neuroticism. I did not read this paper. (Source 5)
- Type-based and trait-based descriptions may capture different information. Kerber et al. note person-centred type models can be more useful for outcomes tied to several traits at once. (Source 17)
- Publisher-run research is not automatically wrong; the problem is that independent replication is thin.

## Open questions a new study could answer

1. If 1,000 or more adults take a Form M or equivalent MBTI and a NEO PI-R/BFI-2 on two occasions, do the four MBTI scales add significant incremental variance (delta R-squared above .02) in a prospective supervisor-rated performance criterion beyond the Big Five?
2. In a preregistered sample, does the proportion of people keeping the same four-letter type at 5 weeks and 12 months exceed 70 percent when scored continuously then cut at the midpoint, versus the same cut on Big Five composite scores of equal reliability?
3. Do MBTI score distributions on E-I, S-N, T-F and J-P show bimodality (dip test p below .05) in a sample over 10,000 when scored with item-response methods, or are they unimodal?
4. Does adding a Neuroticism measure to MBTI scales raise prediction of job satisfaction or burnout above MBTI alone by more than a stated effect size (e.g. delta R-squared above .05)?
5. Do randomly assigned MBTI-style type descriptions (not matched to the person's result) get accuracy ratings within 10 percent of descriptions matched to the person's own result, in a blinded design?
6. Do Gerlach's four clusters assigned from Big Five data explain more variance in a defined outcome (e.g. job satisfaction, N above 5,000) than the 16 MBTI types assigned from the same people?
7. Do teams composed with MBTI diversity differ in preregistered performance outcomes from randomly composed teams, controlling for team mean Conscientiousness and minimum Agreeableness (Bell 2007)?
8. Do occupational self-selection ratios for MBTI types remain stable across independent national samples, and do the same ratios hold for Big Five profiles?
