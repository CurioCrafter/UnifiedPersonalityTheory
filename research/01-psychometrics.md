# Psychometrics of the MBTI: what the evidence shows

Research strand 01. Compiled 2026-10-31. Method note: most journal articles here were paywalled (Wiley, APA and SAGE returned 403 or redirects, and a PDF of Randall et al. was unreadable). Claims therefore rest on abstracts, library records and search-result summaries, and are flagged as such in the Sources list. Treat every number as "as reported in the abstract or summary" until checked against the full paper. No source marked "full text read" is a peer-reviewed journal article; those are publisher/foundation web pages and a PubMed abstract page.

## Bottom line

The scale scores of the MBTI (continuous preference scores) show acceptable internal consistency and, in the best-known meta-analysis, acceptable test-retest correlations (about .80 to .87), with Thinking-Feeling the weakest scale. The weak points are about *types*: critics report that a large share of people (figures of 35% to 50% over about 4 to 5 weeks appear in secondary summaries) receive a different four-letter type on retest, and several independent analyses find no evidence that scores fall into two natural categories (taxometric work found continuous dimensions; the bimodality that early work and some IRT scoring showed was reported as a scoring artifact). The four scales do not map onto Jung's theory as McCrae and Costa read it, but four of them correlate with four Big Five factors, with no coverage of Neuroticism. The best-supported honest summary is: reasonable as a four-scale trait-like questionnaire, poorly supported as a typology. Much of the favourable evidence comes from the publisher or publisher-affiliated outlets, and the independent psychometric literature on structure and type stability is thinner and older than its reputation suggests.

## Findings

### 1. Test-retest reliability of scale scores

- Pooled across studies, scale-score reliability is acceptable, roughly .80 to .87, with Thinking-Feeling lower and more variable (.64 to .87). [Moderate] This is Capraro & Capraro (2002), a reliability generalization meta-analysis; I saw the numbers only through publisher-affiliated summaries (Sources 2, 9), not the paper.
- A later systematic review found 221 candidate studies, only 7 meeting inclusion criteria, with just 3 studies poolable for test-retest and substantial heterogeneity. It reports E-I, S-N and J-P at .75 or higher and T-F at .61. [Moderate] (Source 3, abstract only via search snippet.) Note the T-F weakness appears in both meta-analyses.
- The publisher's current manual reports test-retest coefficients of .81 to .86 on all four scales for 1,721 adults retested 6 to 15 weeks apart (as relayed by the Myers & Briggs Foundation). [Weak] Publisher-only, no method detail visible (Source 8).
- A 2025 review of 193 Form M studies (1999 to 2024; 57,170 participants) found no test-retest studies in its sample and no structural-validity studies outside the manual. [Weak] on its own, but notable. It is published in a counselling journal and summarized on a Foundation-run site; I saw only the Foundation summary (Source 7).

### 2. Type stability (how often four-letter type changes on retest)

- Secondary summaries of Pittenger (1993, 2005) put the share receiving a different four-letter type after about 5 weeks at about 50%, with one cited study at 35% after 4 weeks; another summary gives a range of 39% to 76%. [Contested] The figures come from older studies reviewed by critics, and I could not open the original retest papers. The pro side points out that this is the expected consequence of cutting continuous scores at a midpoint: people scoring near the cut flip. A critic of Pittenger noted he did not report stability by distance from the cut (Source 1, secondary).
- Whole-type stability is a mathematical consequence of scale reliability and of how many respondents sit near the midpoint, so the 50% figure and a .80+ scale correlation are not contradictory. [Moderate] (This is reasoning, not a sourced finding.)
- Percent-same-type figures in the current manual: not visible in any page I could open. Needs checking.

### 3. Internal consistency

- Publisher: each Form M preference scale at .90 or higher (n = 3,009, nationally representative); Form Q facets median .77 (n = 1,378). [Weak] Publisher-only (Source 9).
- Independent-ish: the 2025 Form M synthesis reports internal consistency of .845 to .921 across subscales and total scores, from 10 studies. [Moderate] (Source 7, summary only, and the summarizer is Foundation-affiliated.)
- Caveat: high alpha on a forced-choice, polarized item set says the items hang together, not that the construct is a type.

### 4. Factor structure

- CPP technical briefs state that confirmatory factor analyses favor the four-factor model Myers proposed, citing Harvey, Murry & Stamoulis (1995) and Johnson & Saunders (1990). [Weak] I saw only the briefs' characterization, not fit statistics. (Source 10.)
- The Harvey, Murry & Stamoulis (1995) paper is titled "Unresolved Issues in the Dimensionality of the MBTI" and reports confirmatory analyses of 94 items; I could not see its results (Source 11, record only). The title itself signals open questions.
- McCrae & Costa (1989; n = 267 men, 201 women, ages 19 to 93; NEO-PI self-reports and peer ratings) found four fairly independent dimensions, not dichotomous types, with the scales tracking four of the Big Five (E, O, A, C), no Neuroticism analogue, and doubts about how J-P was interpreted. [Strong] for the "four correlated-with-Big-Five scales" part; replicated by Furnham (1996; n = 160 adults), who found E-I with Extraversion, S-N with Openness, T-F with Agreeableness, J-P with Conscientiousness and none with Neuroticism (Sources 4, 5; 5 is search snippet only).
- Form M versus Form Q: the two share the 93 Form M items; Q adds 20 facets. I found no independent confirmatory comparison. The Form Q facet structure is [Weak], publisher-only.

### 5. The dichotomy question (categories versus continuous traits)

- Taxometric analysis (MAMBAC and MAXCOV-HITMAX) of the MBTI plus two other Jungian measures found no non-arbitrary taxon; preferences appear to be continuous dimensions (Arnau, Green, Rosen, Gleaves & Melancon, 2003). [Moderate] One independent study across three instruments (Source 6, abstract only; sample sizes not seen).
- McCrae & Costa (1989) reached the same conclusion from a different method. [Moderate] Two independent lines, but both older.
- Bimodality: Early ETS reports were mixed (Lord 1958 noted multimodal distributions in some groups; Stricker & Ross 1962 and Hicks 1984 found no marked bimodality, with platykurtosis). Bess & Harvey (2002; about 12,000 leadership-program participants) report that earlier bimodality arose from the BILOG default of 10 quadrature points; with 50 points, distributions were strongly center-weighted. [Moderate] Single large study; I saw only a search-result summary (Sources 12, 13). The same snippet suggests rankings were not changed by the setting.
- So the evidence tilts to continuous, unimodal scores. [Contested] only in the sense that the proponent response (below) argues categories are still useful for practice even if the construct is continuous.

### 6. Forms M and Q, and publisher versus independent results

- Form M (1998) and Q (2001) use IRT-based scoring and item selection; Form Q adds facets (Source 9, publisher).
- Pattern across what I saw: favourable reliability, alpha and CFA claims trace to CPP/Myers-Briggs Company materials, the Foundation (CAPT-affiliated), or reviews summarized on their sites. Critical claims trace to independent journals (Consulting Psychology Journal, Journal of Personality, Personality and Individual Differences, Social and Personality Psychology Compass). [Contested as a pattern] This is an observation about what I found, not a measured publication-bias estimate. Independent syntheses (Capraro & Capraro; Randall et al.) are broadly moderate-to-favourable on scale scores, so funding source does not simply predict direction.

### 7. Major critical reviews

- Pittenger (2005, Consulting Psychology Journal: Practice and Research 57(3), 210-221): critique centred on retest stability; per secondary sources it also concedes that claims for the superiority of other instruments were premature (Source 1, secondary).
- Boyle (1995, Australian Psychologist 30(1), 71-74): reviews psychometric limitations of reliability and validity, per its abstract (Source 14).
- Stein & Swan (2019, Social and Personality Psychology Compass 13(2)): argue MBTI theory has factual and logical problems and offer a teaching framing; I saw only secondary descriptions (Source 15, secondary).
- NRC (Druckman & Bjork, 1991, In the Mind's Eye): per a secondary description, concluded there was not enough well-designed research to justify using it in career counselling; a contemporary letter disputes that reading (Source 16, secondary). I could not open the MBTI chapter.

## What MBTI proponents argue (strongest honest version)

- Scale scores are reliable enough: pooled retest correlations of about .80 to .87 and alphas of .85 or higher (Capraro & Capraro 2002; 2025 Form M synthesis; publisher manual).
- Type flips on retest are expected for anyone near the cut-off, and the instrument reports a preference clarity index; flipping does not mean scores are noisy. The critique conflates score reliability and category assignment.
- Item response theory scoring in Forms M and Q improves precision near the cut-points, and the Bess & Harvey result shows IRT scoring does not create spurious bimodality.
- The scales correlate sensibly with Big Five factors and with independent behavioural variables (the publisher lists examples; Foundation page, Source 8), so they measure something real even if not Jungian types.
- Categories are a communication and development device, not a metaphysical claim; the Foundation cites Moyle & Hackston (2018) for "experiential validity" (I could not verify that paper; see below).

## Open questions a new study could answer (falsifiable)

1. In a preregistered sample of at least 1,000 people retested at 4 weeks and 12 months with Form M, the proportion keeping all four letters will not differ from the proportion predicted by a continuous-score model with the observed scale reliabilities (null: observed stability equals model-predicted stability).
2. Taxometric analysis (MAMBAC, MAXCOV, L-Mode) of Form M item-level data in at least 3 independent samples will find a comparative curve fit index above .50 for a taxonic model on at least one scale; if none does, the dichotomy claim is falsified for that form.
3. A confirmatory four-factor model with correlated or uncorrelated factors, fit to Form M and Form Q item data by someone with no CPP funding, will reach conventional fit (RMSEA at or below .06, CFI at or above .95) without freeing cross-loadings; if it fails, the structure claim is falsified.
4. Respondents whose scores lie within one clarity band of the cut will show retest type flips at a rate that predicts the behavioural differences of the "opposite" type no worse than respondents with clear preferences; if the behaviour tracks the underlying score, not the category, categories add nothing.
5. The T-F scale's lower retest reliability (.61 in one review) will replicate in a new sample of at least 500; if it reaches .80, the earlier gap was a sampling artifact.
6. Incremental validity: four continuous MBTI scale scores will add no more than 2% of explained variance for job-performance or well-being outcomes over the Big Five (including Neuroticism); if they add more, they carry information the Big Five lacks.
